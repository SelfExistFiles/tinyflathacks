You know the feeling. You move into what the estate agent optimistically calls a "compact Victorian terrace" in a nice part of North London, and when you actually see the kitchen, you realise it was built when people ate bread and margarine for dinner and never, ever cooked. The kitchen in our flat was 1.8 metres wide. I measured it twice. I'm not proud of that.

Three months later, having spent roughly £1,800 and an embarrassing amount of time on Pinterest, we've actually got a kitchen you can stand in without your hips brushing both walls simultaneously. More importantly, we can now cook an actual meal without feeling like we're in a test of human endurance.

Here's what we did, and why it actually worked.

---

## The Problems (There Were Many)

Before we start on the solutions, let me paint a picture of the original state:

![Small kitchen before renovation - cramped, dark, yellow painted walls, minimal counter space](/kitchen_makeover_banner.png)

*The before: not quite a cupboard, but not far off*

The main issues weren't complicated:

- **No counter space.** Literally a 60cm run of Formica that had definitely seen better decades
- **Terrible lighting.** One bare bulb in the ceiling that made everything look like it was filmed for a true crime documentary
- **Random cupboards everywhere.** Not built to any coherent plan—just wedged in wherever they'd fit
- **Absolutely no ventilation.** Which meant every time you boiled an egg, condensation would drip down the windows until approximately Thursday
- **An ancient cooker with two working rings** (out of four, which tells you something)
- **Nowhere to store anything** except inside the cupboards, which had the storage capacity of a cereal box

The real killer, though, was psychological. That kitchen made you *feel* small. You couldn't invite people round. You couldn't cook properly. You definitely couldn't do both at the same time.

---

## What We Actually Changed (And Why)

We had three rules:

1. **No structural changes.** Renting means no moving walls or plumbing. Budget-conscious also means no moving walls or plumbing.
2. **Make it *feel* bigger.** Which is often about light and air more than actual square metres.
3. **Keep it under £2,000.** Because we're not mad.

Here's what we did:

### 1. Ripped Out All the Cupboards and Started Fresh

The original cupboards were a mess of different heights, materials, and questionable carpentry. We kept the basic structure but removed all the doors and most of the internal shelving.

**Cost:** £0 (just our time and a wheelie bin hire at £15)

We then measured properly and bought a mix of IKEA base cabinets and open shelving. Everyone's going to tell you that open shelving is impractical and you'll regret it, and they're partially right. But in a kitchen that's visually claustrophobic, open shelves actually let the eye *travel*, which makes the space feel bigger even if you're a bit messier than you'd like.

**Cabinet cost:** £650

### 2. Added Proper Work Surfaces and Storage

Surprisingly, the answer wasn't more cupboards—it was *better* organisation of the cupboards we had. We got rid of the drawer dividers that came with the IKEA stuff and went with a combination of:

- Small wire baskets for non-perishables (moved to a wall-mounted organiser, keeping counter clear)
- A magnetic knife strip mounted low on the wall (both looks decent and saves space)
- A corner pull-out carousel that actually revolutionised access to the worst cupboard in the house
- Open shelving for everyday plates and bowls instead of stacking them in a cupboard where you can't see them

The work surface itself: we went with a laminate from Howdens instead of the Formica original. Slightly more expensive but still budget-friendly, and it *looks* contemporary rather than "salvaged from a 1970s holiday cottage."

**Storage solutions cost:** £280

### 3. Painted Everything White (I Know, I Know)

The walls were this truly horrific mustard yellow. We understood why—small kitchens supposedly benefit from warm colours. But this wasn't warm. It was oppressive. Like cooking inside a jar of Colman's mustard.

We went with Farrow & Ball's All White (yes, we're those people, but it was actually on offer). The ceiling went glossy white too. This single decision probably did 40% of the heavy lifting in making the space feel bigger.

**Paint cost:** £85

### 4. Lighting. Proper Lighting.

The single bare bulb was a crime against kitchens. We put in:

- A small flush-fit spotlight for actual task lighting (over the hob)
- A strip of LED tape under the upper cupboards for work surface illumination
- Replaced the main ceiling fitting with a thin recessed panel light that doesn't take up any headroom

This isn't just about brightness—it's about eliminating shadows that make a small space feel smaller. When you remove the dark corners, everything reads as bigger.

**Lighting cost:** £180

### 5. Replaced the Cooker

The old one was genuinely dangerous. We got a slim 600mm cooker from John Lewis (£220) instead of the standard 900mm. Yes, you have slightly less hob space, but what we *actually* use about five percent of the time isn't where the compromise hurts.

**Cooker cost:** £220

### 6. Added Ventilation That Actually Works

The original kitchen relied on opening the window and hoping. We installed a compact extractor hood (not even a full-size one—just a 50cm slim model) that vents to the outside through the external wall (we were lucky to have a nearby wall vent).

**Extractor cost:** £145

### 7. Handled the Flooring

Didn't replace it—just properly deep-cleaned the existing lino and sealed it. Sometimes the best budget move is not doing something.

**Floor cost:** £0 (already had the sealer)

## The After (The Bit Where It Pays Off)


*The after: still compact, but now it doesn't make you want to cry*

![Kitchen in use with evening lighting showing how space feels expanded](/kitchen_makeover_after.png)

*Evening lighting really shows the difference—the space breathes*

It's not a showroom. It's not even *that* big. But here's what changed:

- **You can actually cook.** We've done pasta for two. We've done a full Sunday roast prep (admittedly with some strategic plate management). Both felt possible.
- **It *looks* clean even when it's a bit messy.** Because there's less visual clutter and better lighting, a couple of dirty plates don't feel like a disaster zone.
- **You can have someone in there with you without it being weird.** This sounds small, but it fundamentally changed how we use the kitchen.
- **The light is genuinely nice.** That might sound like an odd thing to care about, but spending time in a space with good light is measurably better for mood.

## What Actually Mattered (The Learnings Bit)

If I'm honest, the changes that made the most difference weren't the expensive ones:

1. **Painting it white and bright was about 70% of the win.** That alone made it feel twice the size.
2. **Getting the lighting right was about 20% of the win.** Shadows kill small spaces.
3. **Removing visual clutter through organisation was about 10% of the win.** Open shelving helped, but only because we actually keep it tidy.

The new cooker is lovely. The extractor hood makes an actual difference to the moisture problem. But would we have noticed them if the room was still yellow and lit by a single sad bulb? Probably not.

---

## The Honest Bit

We got lucky in a few ways:

- We didn't need to replace the plumbing or move things around
- We found some decent second-hand handles on eBay (saved about £40)
- We got the Farrow & Ball paint on sale (saved about £40)
- Our landlord was surprisingly flexible about what we changed (the paint was technically negotiable)

If you're in a rental, or if you need structural changes, or if you're building from a worse starting point, this will cost more. If you need a new sink, new taps, or a full rewire, you're probably looking at double this budget at minimum.

But the core principle holds: **a small kitchen doesn't need a massive budget. It needs clarity, light, and honest thinking about what you actually use.**

---

## What We'd Do Differently

- The open shelving looks great but does require a bit more discipline than I naturally possess. If you're messier than me (and most people are), invest a bit more in closed storage and accept you'll feel a bit more crammed.
- We'd probably spend slightly more on the paint—Dulux has some genuinely good whites at a better price point than Farrow & Ball. We were just being a bit precious.
- The 600mm cooker is genuinely fine, but if you entertain regularly or cook ambitious things, stick with 900mm and accept the kitchen will feel tighter. Don't make our mistake of optimising for "feeling bigger" over "can actually cook."

---

## The Takeaway

You don't need much space to have a proper kitchen. What you actually need is enough light, enough air, and enough organisation to not feel like you're cooking in a shoe box.

This isn't a fancy renovation. It's not an Instagram kitchen. But it's a *real* kitchen where we can actually cook proper food, and it cost less than a decent holiday.

That's not a bad result for a cupboard-sized Victorian space and a weekend's work.
