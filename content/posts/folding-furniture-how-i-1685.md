I’ll be honest with you: living in a flat where you can touch both walls of your kitchen by simply stretching your arms out is, well, it’s a bit of a nightmare. My current kitchen is exactly 2.4 metres by 1.8 metres. It’s not a kitchen; it’s a glorified hallway with a hob. 

For the first six months, every time I wanted to cook a decent meal, it turned into a proper faff. If I wanted to open the fridge, I had to stand in the hallway. If I wanted to use the chopping board, I had to balance it precariously over the sink. Honestly, it was doing my head in. I’m not a professional chef, but I do enjoy a bit of cooking, and this setup was turning me into a microwave-meal hermit.

### The Before: A Tiny Disaster

Before I decided to change things, the space was a total mess. I had a tiny, wobbly breakfast bar that took up half the floor space. It was one of those "designer" pieces that looked great in the shop but was completely useless in a real-life scenario. It had permanent tea stains on the surface, and because it was fixed, it meant that if I had one person over, we were essentially performing a choreographed dance just to reach the kettle.

To be fair, I didn't help myself. I kept buying "gadgets"—a spiraliser I used once, an air fryer that occupied an entire square metre, and enough mugs to cater for a small wedding. My kitchen was cluttered, cramped, and frankly, a bit depressing.

### The Plan: Folding is the Future

I decided I needed a change. I spent about £450 on a total rethink, mostly focusing on getting stuff off the floor. The mantra was simple: if I’m not using it, it shouldn't exist in the middle of the room.

The plan was simple enough:
1. Get rid of the bulky fixed table.
2. Install a wall-mounted drop-leaf table.
3. Use folding chairs that can be tucked away behind the door.
4. Get some magnetic storage strips to clear the counters.

I’m no master carpenter, but I’ve got a drill and a healthy dose of stubbornness. I figured if I could hang a shelf, I could handle this.

### The After: A Roomier Reality

The transformation took me a weekend—mostly because I spent four hours trying to find a stud in the wall, only to realise I was drilling into a hollow bit of plasterboard. Classic me.

I picked up a solid oak drop-leaf table for £120. When it’s down, it’s only 15cm deep. When it’s up, it comfortably fits two people for dinner. The difference is staggering. I gained nearly 1.5 square metres of floor space. That might sound like nothing to people living in houses with actual gardens, but in my flat, it’s the difference between feeling like a sardine and feeling like a human being.

I also grabbed two foldable chairs that cost £30 each. They’re surprisingly sturdy. I used to be terrified that they’d collapse the moment I sat down with a bowl of pasta, but they’ve held up perfectly for three months now. When I'm done with them, they hang on a couple of heavy-duty hooks I installed on the back of the pantry door. It’s a proper satisfying feeling when you click everything into place and suddenly have room to actually move.

Honestly, the best part isn’t even the extra space; it’s the lack of clutter. By moving my chopping boards and utensils to the walls using magnetic strips, I’ve freed up the counter space entirely. I’ve gone from having exactly zero space to prep food to having about 80cm of clear, usable worktop. It’s like moving into a mansion.

### Summary: Is it worth the faff?

If you’re living in a tiny flat and you’re feeling the walls closing in, I cannot recommend folding furniture enough. Is it a bit of a mission to install? Yes. Does it require a bit of discipline to actually fold the stuff away after you use it? Also yes.

To be fair, there are days when I’m feeling lazy and I leave the table up, and I end up stubbing my toe on the leg. It’s a recurring theme in my life. But even with the occasional bruised toe, it’s miles better than the permanent clutter I had before.

My advice? Measure your space twice, buy once, and don't be afraid to drill into your walls. Just make sure you’ve got a stud finder that actually works, unlike the cheap one I bought for a fiver. 

Living in a small space doesn't have to mean living in a messy one. It just takes a bit of planning and a willingness to get your hands dirty. Now, if you’ll excuse me, I’m off to cook a roast—in a kitchen that actually has space for a roasting tin. Who would’ve thought?
